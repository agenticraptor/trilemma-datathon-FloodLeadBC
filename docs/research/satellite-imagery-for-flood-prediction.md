> **About this document:** an AI-assisted research report (Claude, Oct 9, 2026) on whether satellite imagery with computer-vision or vision-language models, or AI weather models, can give FloodLead an edge in flood timing and accuracy. Every claim links to its source; pass times and latencies were measured from public catalogues on Oct 9, 2026. **Decision recorded in `docs/build/PLAN.md`:** no satellite imagery or vision models as forecast inputs; AI rainfall forecasts tested as a labelled one-event case study and then archived live; Canada's free EGS flood polygons used only to show where water went.

# Skip satellite vision; test AI rainfall instead

FloodLead BC should not use satellite imagery, live or archived, with computer-vision or vision-language ("agentic") models to forecast when or how high the Nooksack will flood Sumas Prairie: revisit gaps, processing delays and winter cloud make satellites a confirmation layer that arrives after the gauges, and in both recent floods no satellite image showed the overflow before the gauges did, while the first satellite maps of the flooded Canadian side were made after the water arrived. The best-timed free radar pass of December 2025 imaged the area 3.4 hours after the Emerson Road overflow gauge began rising, yet the scene became downloadable only at midnight, about an hour after Abbotsford's evacuation order; Canada's own radar flood map was acquired the following evening, after the City had reported water crossing the border. The research behind this report found no study showing that satellite inputs improve 1–48-hour threshold timing in a gauged, rain-driven basin like the Nooksack, and the model evidence is blunt: vision-language models answer about 40% of geospatial benchmark questions correctly and do worse on radar, while a plain U-Net still beats the geospatial foundation models on the standard flood-mapping benchmark. Archived imagery has one honest job: Canada's free radar flood polygons (24 products for 2021, 11 for 2025) show where overflow water went, which can support FloodLead's farm-zone messages but not its forecast. The more credible "AI edge" is AI weather prediction, because forecast rainfall is the largest source of error beyond about a day; ECMWF's AIFS, NOAA's AIGFS family and Google's WeatherNext 3 are free or free on request, but published verifications show AI global models under-forecasting heavy rain and atmospheric-river moisture, none has been verified in the Pacific Northwest, and only December 2025 can be replayed with an operational AI model's archived forecasts, provided a public archive holds them at fixed lead times. Neither idea can be proven before the October 13 demo at any budget, so the minimal honest step is a $0, roughly one-worker-day, pre-registered case study comparing AIFS with IFS, HRDPS, HRRR and NBM rainfall forecasts 24 and 48 hours before the December 2025 overflow, followed by a live, scored shadow archive of AI ensembles through the 2026–27 storm season. Commercial radar tasking ($1,000–15,000 per event), VLM image reading or self-hosted weather models would buy demo theatre, not skill.

## The best-timed radar pass of 2025 went public after the evacuation order

Only radar can image a flood through an atmospheric river's cloud, and the free radar covering Sumas Prairie now looks four times every 12 days. ESA planned to phase out Sentinel-1A once Sentinel-1C and the new Sentinel-1D settled into a paired orbit in July 2026 ([CDSE](https://dataspace.copernicus.eu/node/3176)), and none of the **12 Sentinel-1 acquisitions over a Sumas Prairie test box between September 1 and October 9, 2026** came from it: Sentinel-1C passes near 6:00 PM and Sentinel-1D passes near 6:15 AM (winter clock time), with gaps alternating between about 2.5 and 4.5 days ([ASF search API](https://api.daac.asf.alaska.edu/services/search/param)). Each scene then needs hours to become usable. In December 2025, processed Sentinel-1 scenes appeared on the Copernicus Data Space **2.3–6.1 hours after sensing**, and both flood-day scenes took the full 6.1 hours, most of it before ground processing began ([CDSE catalogue](https://catalogue.dataspace.copernicus.eu/odata/v1/Products)). Copernicus Global Flood Monitoring (GFM) turns each scene into a 20 m flood map but promises delivery within 8 hours only when the scene reaches ESA's hubs within 5 ([GFM 2026 overview](https://global-flood.emergency.copernicus.eu/media/events/9/gfm_intro_glofas_am_2026-tobias.pdf); [GFM manual](https://extwiki.eodc.eu/GFM/PUM/TechnicalOverview)). NASA's analysis-ready OPERA backscatter for the two December 2025 flood passes landed 19.0 and 9.5 hours after sensing; OPERA's water-extent maps for the Sumas tile were not produced for those passes until April 2026; and in September–October 2026 OPERA processed none of Sentinel-1D's five passes over the prairie ([CMR, OPERA RTC Dec 2025](https://cmr.earthdata.nasa.gov/search/granules.json?short_name=OPERA_L2_RTC-S1_V1&bounding_box=-122.30,49.00,-122.10,49.10&temporal=2025-12-04T00:00:00Z,2025-12-20T00:00:00Z); [CMR, OPERA DSWx Dec 2025](https://cmr.earthdata.nasa.gov/search/granules.json?short_name=OPERA_L3_DSWX-S1_V1&bounding_box=-122.30,49.00,-122.10,49.10&temporal=2025-12-04T00:00:00Z,2025-12-20T00:00:00Z); [CMR, OPERA RTC 2026](https://cmr.earthdata.nasa.gov/search/granules.json?short_name=OPERA_L2_RTC-S1_V1&bounding_box=-122.30,49.00,-122.10,49.10&temporal=2026-09-10T00:00:00Z,2026-10-09T23:59:59Z)). The other free radars are slower still. NISAR took no image of the basin between December 5 and 15, 2025, and now delivers products 45–61 hours after acquisition; SWOT's river heights arrived 77–104 hours after sensing ([CMR, NISAR beta](https://cmr.earthdata.nasa.gov/search/granules.json?short_name=NISAR_L2_GCOV_BETA_V1&bounding_box=-122.50,48.75,-122.00,49.10&temporal=2025-10-01T00:00:00Z,2026-02-01T00:00:00Z); [CMR, NISAR provisional](https://cmr.earthdata.nasa.gov/search/granules.json?short_name=NISAR_L2_GCOV_PROVISIONAL_V1&bounding_box=-122.50,48.75,-122.00,49.10&temporal=2026-08-15T00:00:00Z,2026-10-09T23:59:59Z); [CMR, SWOT](https://cmr.earthdata.nasa.gov/search/granules.json?short_name=SWOT_L2_HR_RiverSP_reach_D&bounding_box=-122.50,48.75,-122.00,49.10&temporal=2025-12-01T00:00:00Z,2025-12-25T00:00:00Z)).

Against a flood that builds in hours, that schedule makes a timely look a matter of luck. If an overflow's timing is unrelated to the orbit, four looks per 288 hours give a **38% chance that any free radar look falls inside a 27-hour overflow like December 2025's, 61% for a 44-hour overflow like November 2021's, and about 8% that one lands in the first six hours**, before the publishing delay is added. These figures are arithmetic from the measured pass schedule, with overflow durations reconstructed from the SR 544 overflow gauge record ([USGS, Nov 2021](https://nwis.waterservices.usgs.gov/nwis/iv/?format=rdb&sites=12211200,12211190,12211195,12215100,12214550,12215000,12214500&parameterCd=00065,00060&startDT=2021-11-13T00:00-08:00&endDT=2021-11-19T00:00-08:00); [USGS, Dec 2025](https://nwis.waterservices.usgs.gov/nwis/iv/?format=rdb&sites=12211200,12211190,12211195,12215100,12214550,12215000,12214500&parameterCd=00065,00060&startDT=2025-12-08T00:00-08:00&endDT=2025-12-14T00:00-08:00)). Both floods played out accordingly.

In 2021 the first satellite views of the flood, Canada's RADARSAT Constellation Mission (RCM) image at 6:05 PM on November 15 and Sentinel-1B at 6:20 AM on November 16, were taken after barns were already underwater; the Sentinel-1B pass came about two hours after dairy farmer Chelsea Meier woke to water at 4 AM ([CBC](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/british-columbia/meier-farm-sumas-prairie-recover-floods-abbotsford-warning-system-failure-1.6268183)). In 2025 one radar pass was about as well timed as the schedule allows: Sentinel-1A imaged the area at 5:54 PM on December 10, **3.4 hours after the Emerson Road gauge began rising**. The scene became downloadable at midnight. By then the overflow gauges had been rising for 9.5 hours, NWS had called the overflow "imminent or occurring" more than 13 hours earlier, and Abbotsford had ordered 371 properties out about an hour before ([IEM, 2025 warnings](https://mesonet.agron.iastate.edu/cgi-bin/afos/retrieve.py?pil=FLWSEW&sdate=2025-11-28&edate=2025-12-22&fmt=text&limit=600); [Abbotsford News](https://abbynews.com/2025/12/10/evacuation-order-issued-for-clayburn-village-sumas-prairie-west/)). The only image taken during the storm before the overflow, at 6:01 PM on December 9, went public about two hours after NWS's first North Cedarville warning had already named Abbotsford. Sentinel-1 then missed the December 11–12 peak; its next look came on December 14. Canada's Emergency Geomatics Service (EGS) covered the peak with RCM imagery, but its first 2025 acquisition, at 5:58 PM on December 11, came after the City's 11 AM bulletin had reported water crossing and after the Vye Road dike breached that afternoon. Even at EGS's four-hour target it could not have been public before about 10 PM, and the archive copy is stamped mid-morning on December 12 ([EGS archive 2025](https://data.eodms-sgdot.nrcan-rncan.gc.ca/public/EGS/2025/Flood/CAN/BC/); [Active Floods in Canada](https://open.canada.ca/data/en/dataset/9cad712a-5ac5-4248-b7d7-2db1a3892509)). The live EGS service may have been faster than the archive stamps suggest, but no product can precede its own acquisition.

Optical imagery is worse. Across eight November–December seasons from 2017 to 2025 (2018 missing), **47% of 194 Sentinel-2 scenes of the Sumas tile were at least 90% cloud and only 15% were under 20%**; every one of the 15 optical acquisitions between December 4 and 22, 2025 was at least 68% cloud ([CDSE catalogue](https://catalogue.dataspace.copernicus.eu/odata/v1/Products); [CMR, DSWx-HLS](https://cmr.earthdata.nasa.gov/search/granules.json?short_name=OPERA_L3_DSWX-HLS_V1&bounding_box=-122.30,49.00,-122.10,49.10&temporal=2025-12-04T00:00:00Z,2025-12-20T00:00:00Z)). NOAA's VIIRS flood maps arrive within about an hour but come from daytime optical passes, so "clouds prevent viewing of the surface" ([CIRA quick guide](https://ramsdis.cira.colostate.edu/rammb/training/visit/quick_guides/VIIRS_Flood_Areal_Extent_Quick_Guide_v4.pdf)). Commercial radar changes the revisit but not the logic. ICEYE claims four or more looks a day and flood-extent updates every six hours ([ICEYE](https://www.iceye.com/floodlive)) at list prices of $1,050–3,750 per tasked scene and $450 per archive scene; Umbra charges $675–3,750 per scene and Capella $13.85–166 per km² for tasking ([SkyFi pricing](https://skyfi.com/files/SkyFi_Pricing_August_2025.pdf); [SkyWatch, Capella](https://skywatch.com/geospatial-data-providers/capella-space/)). One to four tasked scenes per event would cost roughly **$1,000–15,000** (estimate from list prices), and even six-hourly extent maps after onset cannot beat overflow gauges that report every 15 minutes. If the worry is gauge failure, and the Emerson Road overflow gauge was indeed out of service on October 9, 2026 ([NWPS NEEW1](https://api.water.noaa.gov/nwps/v1/gauges/NEEW1)), the remedy is the SR 544 gauge, the two USGS overflow webcams and a repaired gauge ([Whatcom County](https://www.whatcomcounty.us/594/Early-Flood-Warning)), not a sensor that looks every three days.

## Satellite inputs barely move forecasts where gauges already exist

Satellites could still matter indirectly, as inputs that sharpen a forecast model rather than as flood detectors. The literature says the gain is small exactly where FloodLead operates. A diagnostic study of SMAP soil-moisture assimilation found that **about 60% of random runoff error comes directly from rainfall and cannot be corrected** by updating soil moisture, that fast-response runoff "is relatively unresponsive to state updating", and that total runoff improved by under 10% even in the synthetic best case ([Mao, Crow & Nijssen, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1175/JHM-D-18-0115.1)). When the Copernicus Global Flood Awareness System (GloFAS) was tested with SMOS soil-moisture assimilation in its forcing chain, skill "was only affected by a small amount" ([Baugh et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/rs12091490)). Larger gains, 27–31% lower ensemble error, came from a semi-arid Australian catchment of more than 40,000 km², a setting with little in common with a wet, steep, gauged Cascades basin ([Alvarez-Garreton et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.5194/hess-19-1659-2015)).

Assimilating radar flood maps into hydraulic models helps more, but in a setting FloodLead does not share. On the River Severn, flood-map assimilation cut forecast error by as much as 50% at the assimilation time ([Hostache et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1029/2017WR022205)), and a later study using a tempered particle filter cut water-level error 80% at assimilation and 60% two days later, with gains lasting almost three days ([Di Mauro et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1029/2022WR031940)). Those comparisons are against a model that receives no updates at all, and much of the work is synthetic ([Di Mauro et al., 2021](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/25/4081/2021/)). The first real-event test of SWOT altimetry shows what happens next to gauges: on the Garonne in 2024, **assimilating SWOT alone made gauge-point errors worse (0.449 to 0.491 m at Tonneins, 0.195 to 0.383 m at La Réole), while assimilating the gauges cut them to 0.116 and 0.068 m** ([SWOT reanalysis, 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.21670v1)). Satellite updates help models that lack gauges; FloodLead's model is built on gauges.

Operational centres behave accordingly. NOAA's National Water Model v3.0 added 678 stream gauges to its assimilation and its change notice mentions no satellite data ([NWS SCN 23-76](https://www.weather.gov/media/notification/pdf_2023_24/scn23-76_national_water_model_v3.0_aab.pdf)), while NWS River Forecast Centers use automated VIIRS flood maps for monitoring, not as model inputs ([Li et al., 2018](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/48455)). Google's global flood model is the only operational system found that feeds a satellite product, NASA's IMERG rainfall, into its streamflow forecasts, but it runs at a daily step and its paper reports no test of what IMERG contributes ([Nearing et al., 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07145-1)); its 2026 successor adds GraphCast weather forcing, and a public comment on the preprint notes that sub-daily peaks are not evaluated ([Cohen et al., 2026](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2026/egusphere-2026-2283/)). Google's hourly stage system, which used IMERG with a 12-hour delay, did not yet handle flash floods or basins under 1,000 km² ([Nevo et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.02780)).

The individual satellite inputs available for the Nooksack confirm the pattern. IMERG Early arrives 4.3–4.8 hours after the rain on a 0.1° grid (about 81 km² per cell at this latitude), its gauge-calibrated Final run has not yet reached December 2025, and over the nearby Olympic Mountains satellite products including IMERG were found to under-estimate orographic rain by 57% ([CMR, IMERG Early](https://cmr.earthdata.nasa.gov/search/granules.json?short_name=GPM_3IMERGHHE&version=07&bounding_box=-122.50,48.75,-122.00,49.10&temporal=2025-12-10T12:00:00Z,2025-12-10T13:00:00Z); [CMR, IMERG Final](https://cmr.earthdata.nasa.gov/search/granules.json?short_name=GPM_3IMERGHH&version=07&bounding_box=-122.50,48.75,-122.00,49.10&temporal=2025-01-01T00:00:00Z,2026-10-09T00:00:00Z&sort_key=-start_date&page_size=1); [Hunzinger et al., NASA NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20180008571/downloads/20180008571.pdf)). SMAP's near-real-time soil moisture arrives 0.7–1.8 hours after each of about two daily passes, but on a 36 km grid, one cell larger than Sumas Prairie ([CMR, SMAP NRT](https://cmr.earthdata.nasa.gov/search/collections.json?short_name=SPL2SMP_NRT); [CMR, SMAP passes](https://cmr.earthdata.nasa.gov/search/granules.json?short_name=SPL2SMP&bounding_box=-122.50,48.75,-122.00,49.10&temporal=2025-12-09T00:00:00Z,2025-12-13T00:00:00Z)). Cloud-gap-filled VIIRS snow cover estimates what "may exist under current cloud cover" from the last clear day, and MODIS begins shutting down in late 2026 or early 2027 ([CMR, VIIRS snow](https://cmr.earthdata.nasa.gov/search/collections.json?short_name=VNP10A1F); [NASA Earthdata](https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/data/alerts-outages/transition-from-modis-viirs)). GOES-18 images the region every 5–10 minutes, but its precipitable-water product works only in clear sky, so an atmospheric river's moisture under cloud needs microwave composites such as the hourly MIMIC-TPW ([NOAA STAR](https://www.star.nesdis.noaa.gov/atmospheric-composition-training/satellite_data_abi_scan_modes.php); [data.gov, ABI TPW](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/noaa-goes-r-series-advanced-baseline-imager-abi-level-2-total-precipitable-water-tpw); [CIMSS](https://tropic.ssec.wisc.edu/misc/mimictpw/info.html)). None of these addresses the error that matters most, the rain that has not yet fallen. NWS's first crest forecasts at North Cedarville were about 2 ft low roughly a day ahead in both floods, 148.9 ft against 150.76 ft in 2021 and 148.4 ft against 150.49 ft in 2025, and forecast rainfall was the largest error source in the only public NWRFC verification case study ([IEM, 2021 warnings](https://mesonet.agron.iastate.edu/cgi-bin/afos/retrieve.py?pil=FLWSEW&sdate=2021-11-10&edate=2021-11-20&fmt=text&limit=400); [IEM, 2025 warnings](https://mesonet.agron.iastate.edu/cgi-bin/afos/retrieve.py?pil=FLWSEW&sdate=2025-11-28&edate=2025-12-22&fmt=text&limit=600); [NWPS NRKW1](https://api.water.noaa.gov/nwps/v1/gauges/NRKW1); [NWRFC case study](https://www.weather.gov/media/owp/oh/rfcdev/docs/NWRFC_national_verification_case_study_RFCworkshop_Nov08_Part1.pdf)). The research found no study, for any basin of this kind, that measures how satellite inputs change the timing of 1–48-hour threshold crossings, and none for the Nooksack or Fraser Valley.

## Segmentation models map water; vision-language models misread it

If FloodLead uses imagery at all, it should consume the maps others already make. In the inventory below, ordered by usefulness to FloodLead, the free Canadian and Copernicus products beat any pipeline a small team could build in three days.

For turning radar scenes into water maps, the model question is close to settled, and it does not favour novelty. On the Sen1Floods11 benchmark a supervised **U-Net scored 91.42 mIoU, ahead of every foundation model in the PANGAEA comparison** (CROMA 90.89, Prithvi 1.0 90.37, RemoteCLIP 74.26), and it kept the lead with only 10% of the labels ([PANGAEA](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.04204v1)). IBM and ESA's Apache-2.0 TerraMind reports 81.06 mIoU from Sentinel-1 radar and 89.70 from Sentinel-2 optical on the same dataset ([TerraMind](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.11171)). Radar alone is the hard case, and it is the only case that matters under atmospheric-river cloud. On the 19-event FloodPlanet benchmark, **Sentinel-1 scores were 0.48–0.57 mIoU against 0.62–0.79 for optical and PlanetScope imagery**; Prithvi 2.0 led on radar, while Clay v1.5 generalised best across regions (0.73 ± 0.10 against U-Net's 0.69 ± 0.22) and with only five labelled images, in single-seed results the authors call "small-to-moderate" gains ([Kaushik et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.01990)). Compute is no barrier: a Sen1Floods11-tuned Prithvi-EO-2.0 checkpoint is published under Apache-2.0, the model family fine-tunes on a single GPU, and Clay trains at about 1.6 minutes per epoch on one RTX A6000 ([Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/ibm-nasa-geospatial/Prithvi-EO-2.0-300M-TL-Sen1Floods11); [Prithvi-EO-2.0](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.02732); [Kaushik et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.01990)). Google's AlphaEarth is a different tool: annual 10 m embeddings that, combined with population data, raised R² for riverine flood-risk mapping by 17%, useful for static exposure and useless for detecting a flood in progress ([Earth Engine catalog](https://developers.google.com/earth-engine/datasets/catalog/GOOGLE_SATELLITE_EMBEDDING_V1_ANNUAL); [Google Research](https://www.research.google/blog/google-earth-ai-unlocking-geospatial-insights-with-foundation-models-and-cross-modal-reasoning/)). One local caution applies to any segmenter: GFM excludes dense vegetation and urban areas, lists agricultural fields among radar water look-alikes and targets a critical success index of at least 70% ([GFM manual](https://extwiki.eodc.eu/GFM/PUM/TechnicalOverview); [GFM 2021 introduction](https://european-flood.emergency.copernicus.eu/sites/default/files/2021-10/2021_CEMS_GFM_Introduction.pdf)), so on Sumas Prairie's farmland any model would need checking against the EGS polygons before anyone trusted it.

Vision-language models are the wrong instrument for measurement. On GEOBench-VLM (31 geospatial tasks, over 10,000 questions) **the best model averaged 41.7% on multiple choice, about twice random guessing**, GPT-4o did slightly worse, and GPT-4o's referring-expression detection scored a precision of 0.0087 ([GEOBench-VLM](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.19325)). On DisasterM3, a NeurIPS 2025 benchmark of before-and-after disaster images that includes floods, GPT-4.1 averaged 42.3, Qwen2.5-VL-72B 40.5 and GPT-4o 39.3, while remote-sensing VLMs scored 10.7–23.0; performance was "substantially diminished" on radar, where one model's segmentation fell from 27.5 to 10.9 mIoU, and flood-category accuracy for the models shown was 18.4–27.0 ([DisasterM3](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.21089v2)). A qualitative hydrology test found models offering "arbitrary" discharge values of 900 and 1,000 cfs for the same dam image ([Kadiyala et al.](https://eartharxiv.org/repository/object/7176/download/13733/)). The research found no published scores for current Claude or Gemini models on satellite flood benchmarks, so their performance is unmeasured rather than proven bad, but nothing suggests a chat model reads a radar scene better than a 91-mIoU U-Net. The "agentic" systems that work use the language model as a dispatcher. Google's Gemini-based Geospatial Reasoning agent scores 0.82 against 0.50 for Gemini 2.5 Pro alone on Google's own benchmark because it calls Earth Engine, BigQuery and specialist models, and it is available only through early access ([Google Research](https://www.research.google/blog/google-earth-ai-unlocking-geospatial-insights-with-foundation-models-and-cross-modal-reasoning/)). For FloodLead that pattern is a reasonable post-demo feature: an agent that watches the EGS service and GFM, intersects new polygons with subscribers' farm zones and adds "satellite-confirmed flooding near you, image taken 5:58 PM" to a message. It adds plumbing and plain language, not foresight.

Where satellites earn a place is FloodLead's last-mile problem, which is spatial: which farms the overflow reaches, and in what order. Gauges cannot answer that, the provincial hydraulic modelling Abbotsford relies on is not published, and the model in the new Sumas River watershed initiative is still being built ([City of Abbotsford, Dec 10, 2025](https://www.abbotsford.ca/city-hall/news-media/update-heavy-rain-and-flood-warning); [City of Abbotsford, 2026](https://www.abbotsford.ca/city-hall/news-media/supporting-flood-mitigation-planning-sumas-prairie)), but the EGS archive partly can. It holds 23 Lower Fraser products from November 16 to December 13, 2021, often two a day, and 11 products from December 12 to 21, 2025, at 5–50 m, covering Sumas Prairie and in some cases extending into Whatcom County, all free under the Open Government Licence ([EGS archive 2021](https://data.eodms-sgdot.nrcan-rncan.gc.ca/public/EGS/2021/Flood/CAN/BC/); [EGS archive 2025](https://data.eodms-sgdot.nrcan-rncan.gc.ca/public/EGS/2025/Flood/CAN/BC/)). Sentinel-1B's November 16, 2021 scene, Umbra's two December 19, 2025 scenes between Everson and Sumas, and the Maxar images of November 19, 2021 add independent views ([ASF search API](https://api.daac.asf.alaska.edu/services/search/param); [Umbra bucket](https://umbra-open-data-catalog.s3.amazonaws.com/?list-type=2&prefix=sar-data/tasks/Washington_State_Atmospheric_River_DEC-2025/); [Global News](https://globalnews.ca/news/8389997/bc-flood-satellite-images/amp)). Overlaid on farm zones, they show which areas flooded in each event and how the water spread after the peak, which helps word zone-specific messages and check arrival-order assumptions. They cannot time arrival, because the first images came at or after the crossing, and their relevance will fade once the planned 2026 replacement of the SR 544 culvert with a bridge changes how much water spills north ([WA Ecology](https://ecology.wa.gov/ecologys-work-near-you/river-basins-groundwater/nooksack-transboundary-flood-initiative)). They are zone validation, not training labels for timing.

## AI weather models target the right error, over days rather than hours

Beyond the few hours that upstream gauges buy, the lead time farmers need comes from rainfall forecasts. Observation-only machine-learning models lose skill sharply after about 24 hours, and rainfall forecasts misplace storms worst over small basins ([Liu et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.5194/hess-26-265-2022); [IIHR](https://iihr.uiowa.edu/all-news/2025/10/rainfall-forecast-good-or-bad-flood-forecasting-answer-scale-dependent)). Because forecast rainfall was the largest error source in the NWRFC case study ([NWRFC case study](https://www.weather.gov/media/owp/oh/rfcdev/docs/NWRFC_national_verification_case_study_RFCworkshop_Nov08_Part1.pdf)), it is also the most plausible cause of the 2-ft low crests of 2021 and 2025 (an inference; NWS has published no diagnosis of either forecast), which makes AI weather models the right lever in principle. Three AI forecast streams are now free or free on request, alongside free high-resolution physics models that any AI model must beat.

The evidence says AI global models are more likely to deepen the low-crest problem than fix it, unless their rain is corrected. Comparing GraphCast with GFS and IFS over the continental US for 1–7-day precipitation, Radford, Ebert-Uphoff and Stewart found GraphCast better on RMSE and on threat scores at lower thresholds, but it **"less frequently predicts intense precipitation events" and shows "large negative biases at high accumulation thresholds"** ([NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10596371-comparison-ai-weather-prediction-numerical-weather-prediction-models-day-precipitation-forecasts)). A 32-month comparison found that a high-resolution physics model beat the AI model on extreme precipitation only at short lead times, which is the window that matters here; the abstract does not name either model ([Loveday & Hertneky](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.25045)). A preliminary 2026 evaluation of NeuralGCM, GraphCast, Pangu-Weather and Keisler found "a systematic negative IVT bias that grows with lead time", meaning the models smooth the moisture transport that defines an atmospheric river, although they often kept the river's shape at medium leads ([RMetS poster](https://www.rmets.org/conference/weather-and-climate-conference-2026/poster-presentations/how-well-do-machine-learning)). GenCast's Nature paper left precipitation out of its main results for lack of confidence in ERA5 rain ([Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-08252-9)); ECMWF's AIFS model card lists blurring and "reduced intensity of some high-impact systems" ([AIFS model card](https://huggingface.co/ecmwf/aifs-single-1.0)); and NOAA has published no precipitation verification for AIGFS, only a map of the December 2025 Pacific Northwest storm ([NOAA](https://www.noaa.gov/news-release/noaa-deploys-new-generation-of-ai-driven-global-weather-models)). On the plus side, NOAA reports that its AI ensemble extends skill by 18–24 hours in early testing and that the hybrid ensemble beats both parents ([ForecastWatch](https://forecastwatch.com/2026/01/06/noaa-launches-new-suite-of-weather-models/)). Physics models carry their own regional weakness: a 2018 study found their atmospheric-river occurrence skill at 1–7 days "significantly lower in British Columbia and Alaska" than farther south ([CW3E](https://cw3e.ucsd.edu/?p=11248)). No AI weather model has published verification for Pacific Northwest atmospheric rivers or BC basins.

The two most promising developments cannot be proven this week. WeatherNext 3 is the only AI feed with hourly rain on a 0.1° grid and a fresh 64-member run every hour, and Google claims precipitation CRPS gains of up to 60% against IMERG, 30% against MRMS radar and 10% against rain gauges at early lead times ([Google](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/google-deepmind/introducing-weathernext-3/)). The shrinking gain as the reference improves is a warning in mountains, where satellite rain products carry their largest errors ([Earth and Space Science, 2022](https://doaj.org/article/a1dda0a814ec44e5ab3840d68be78de2)). Access takes 5–7 business days after a request, the real-time data falls under "experimental" terms, the previous generation is labelled "not for operational use", and the archive does not yet cover either flood ([WeatherNext access](https://developers.google.com/weathernext/guides/access-forecast); [WeatherNext models](https://developers.google.com/weathernext/guides/models); [Earth Engine, WeatherNext 2](https://developers.google.com/earth-engine/datasets/catalog/projects_gcp-public-data-weathernext_assets_weathernext_2_0_0)). CW3E's stretched-grid AI model at about 2 km over the western US reproduces observed rain-intensity distributions and sharper coastal rain bands, which is exactly what the Nooksack needs, but its code and weights are unreleased ([Rouzegari et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.25512)). For the first six hours, ground radar beats satellites in the lowlands: ECCC's S-band Aldergrove radar has run since September 2021 with a 240 km Doppler range, and the US MRMS mosaic includes Canadian radars, although the Camano Island radar is blocked to the west and beam blockage leaves gaps across the mountainous Northwest ([ECCC](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/weather-general-tools-resources/radar-overview/modernizing-network.html); [NSSL](https://inside.nssl.noaa.gov/mrms/news/); [UW](https://atmos.uw.edu/~cliff/coastalradar.html); [AMS](https://ams.confex.com/ams/90annual/webprogram/Paper163622.html)). The one place where satellite imagery plus computer vision has a credible mechanism is rain nowcasting: NVIDIA's open StormScope learns storm evolution from geostationary imagery and radar at 6 km and 10-minute steps for 0–6 hours, and Google's Global MetNet nowcasts from satellite data but offers no public feed ([NVIDIA Research](https://research.nvidia.com/publication/2026-01_learning-accurate-storm-scale-evolution-observations); [Global MetNet](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.13050v1)). StormScope is built for the continental US, its coverage of the Nooksack is unconfirmed and it has not been tested on atmospheric rivers, which makes it a candidate for next winter, not this week.

Testability is the binding constraint. FloodLead's pre-registered evaluation holds out November 2021 and December 2025, but no operational AI forecast is archived at fixed lead times for November 2021: Open-Meteo's AIFS series starts February 20, 2025 and its AIGFS series January 7, 2026 ([Open-Meteo Historical Forecast](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs/historical-forecast-api)). Open-Meteo's Previous Runs API, which returns the value a model forecast 24–168 hours before each valid time, holds most models from January 2024 and lists AIFS Single, IFS, HRDPS, HRRR and NBM, but it does not give AIFS's start date, so December 2025 must be checked before anyone promises a replay ([Open-Meteo Previous Runs](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs/previous-runs-api)). AIFS on Open-Meteo is 6-hourly information interpolated to hours ([Open-Meteo ECMWF](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs/ecmwf-api)). The deeper problem is calibration. Correcting an AI model's heavy-rain bias for the Nooksack needs many archived storms, and barely more than one wet season of AIFS forecasts exists in any public archive found. That calibration data can only be created by archiving forecasts as they are issued.

## A $0, one-day case study is the only test that fits before October 13

Satellite-based timing cannot be tested properly before October 13 at any price. The archive holds two floods, the free radar images taken around each one number one to three, and none of them showed the overflow before the gauges did. A proper test of AI rainfall also needs more storms than the archive holds. The plan therefore tests only what can be tested, labels it as a case study, and starts the clock on the real test. It also respects FloodLead's build plan, in which Stage 4's value test runs on October 10, code freezes at noon on October 13 and precipitation forecasts are the first item cut if the build runs late. The write-up reports every model, including those that look bad, calls the result "one event, descriptive", and commits in advance to no product change before the demo whatever it shows. If Stage 4's model takes forecast rain, as its "plus as-issued forecast rain" ablation requires, the same amendment can declare one plug-in run per rain source on the December 2025 holdout, all reported. Because the model was trained on a different rain source, that run is a sensitivity check, not evidence of skill.

The money FloodLead should not spend is easy to name. A home-built satellite segmentation pipeline (Sentinel-1 scenes from the free archives, the published Prithvi checkpoint or a U-Net, checked against EGS polygons) would take roughly 1.5–3 days and under $20 of rented GPU time at $1.99–2.79 per A100-hour ([Lambda](https://lambda.ai/pricing)); both figures are estimates. It would answer "can we map water", which EGS and GFM already answer for free, and not "when will it arrive". Commercial radar tasking for the next storm would cost roughly $1,000–15,000 per event and deliver extent after onset. Self-hosting an AI weather model is unnecessary when AIFS, AIGFS and WeatherNext output is free; a 72-hour, 50-member GenCast-class run would cost roughly $4–5 of GPU time (an estimate scaled from GenCast's published 8 minutes per 15-day forecast on a TPU v5) plus days of data plumbing ([GenCast](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-08252-9)). Commercial optical imagery, at about $5–35 per km² from archive, is blind on flood days ([SkyFi pricing](https://skyfi.com/files/SkyFi_Pricing_August_2025.pdf)). Open-Meteo's free tier covers non-commercial use only, so a commercial FloodLead would need a paid plan or direct feeds from ECMWF and NOAA ([Open-Meteo GEM](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs/gem-api)).

For Demo Day, the strongest AI story is the measured negative result. One slide with the timeline table above, showing when satellites saw each flood against gauge onset, alerts and water on farms, tells judges that FloodLead checked the fashionable idea against timestamps and chose gauges for timing and AI ensembles for the days-ahead problem, with a public scorecard to settle which rain forecast deserves trust. If T1 runs, the slide can add what each model forecast 24 and 48 hours before the 2025 overflow, labelled as one event.

## Conclusion

The decisive variable is not model sophistication but when an observation exists. A gauge at the overflow reports every 15 minutes; a satellite sees the water only if it happens to be overhead, and then hours later, and no computer-vision or language model can recover an observation that was never taken. Satellite flood AI earns its keep on rivers without gauges and in mapping, while the Nooksack overflow has gauges and needs timing. Satellites' real contributions to FloodLead are indirect: a spatial memory of where water went, and the AI nowcasters that learn storm evolution from satellite imagery and reach FloodLead as rain forecasts rather than pictures.

The real AI edge sits upstream of the river model, in choosing which rainfall forecast to trust and how far to inflate its heavy tail, and that question suits FloodLead's public ledger. Logging AIFS, AIGFS, WeatherNext 3, HRDPS, HRRR and NBM rain forecasts as issued, and scoring them against Nooksack gauges storm by storm, would build the first public record of how AI and physics models handle this basin's atmospheric rivers. No agency publishes that record, and it is the only route to crest probabilities that stop repeating the 2-foot low bias of 2021 and 2025. Building it will take a season, not three days, and saying so plainly on October 13 is itself part of the case for trusting FloodLead.
